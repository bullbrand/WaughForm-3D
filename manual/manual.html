1Welcome: what can WaughForm 3D do for me?

WaughForm 3D takes you from an idea to a finished, working gadget. Use as much or as little of it as you like: each step leads naturally into the next.

  1. Draw your own things. Sketch a flat shape, pull it up into 3D, round off the edges, cut holes, sweep, slice. If you've used SketchUp you'll feel at home, and if you haven't, you'll be making real parts in minutes.
  2. Save it and print it. One click checks it's really ready to print (no gaps, nothing floating in mid-air, small enough for your printer) and saves a file your slicer opens straight away.
  3. Starting an electronics project? Choose your parts from over 2,000 ready-made boards, sensors, screens and modules. You don't need to know the part number: search for what it does, like temperature, gyroscope or screen, and add it to your box with one click.
  4. One click, one custom box. The Enclosure Wizard arranges your parts and builds a printable case to hold them: holes lined up with the sockets, buttons and sensors, posts that print without supports, and a lid that fits.
  5. Not sure how to wire it up? Click Wiring. A diagram and a tick-list show exactly which pin goes to which, with safe pins chosen for you and a warning wherever something needs care.
  6. We'll even write the code. WaughForm 3D writes the program for your board. Install the free ESPHome Device Builder on your computer, click Send to ESPHome, and your device appears there: plug the board in and click Install, and ESPHome builds the program and puts it on the board. Tell it what the gadget should do in your own words, and AI fills in the rest.
Projects made simple: your idea, a little thought, and some help from AI.

This manual follows the same path: drawing and printing first (chapters 5 to 7), then parts, the box, wiring and the code (chapters 8 to 12).

2Getting started

Which file to use

You will have one of two files. Either one is the whole program.

FileWhat it does
WaughForm 3D Portable 1.6.1.exeJust double-click it. Nothing is installed, so it also runs from a USB stick.
WaughForm 3D Setup 1.6.1.exeInstalls the program with a Start menu and desktop shortcut. Uninstall it from Settings › Apps.

It needs Windows 10 or 11 (64-bit). Installing a newer version over an older one keeps your drawings, parts and settings.

"Windows protected your PC"

The first time you run it, Windows may show a blue box, because the program isn't signed by a big company. Click More info, then Run anyway. You only need to do this once.

Checking the file is genuine

The file SHA256 checksums that comes with the program lists a long code for each .exe, like a fingerprint. If even one byte of the file is changed (by a virus or a broken download), the code comes out different.

  1. Open the folder that holds the .exe, click in the address bar at the top, type powershell and press Enter.
  2. Type Get-FileHash "WaughForm 3D Portable 1.6.1.exe" and press Enter.
  3. The code it shows must match the one in the checksums file. If it doesn't, don't run the file.
Tip: the codes are also shown on the WaughForm 3D download page on GitHub. Check against those as well: someone who could swap the program could also swap a checksum file sent alongside it.

First thing to do: tell it your printer

Click Printer on the top bar and pick your printer, or type its build size (X, Y and height Z, in mm). The grid then shows your bed, and the app warns you if anything is too big to print.

The view cube: choosing the side you draw on

The cube in the top right corner always turns with your view, so you can see which way you're looking.

  • Click a face of the cube (FRONT, RIGHT, TOP…) to look straight at that side. New shapes are then drawn on that side: a light blue grid shows it, and Drawing on: Front appears under the cube. Click that label to go back to drawing on the ground. TOP means the ground, as usual.
  • Click an edge or a corner of the cube for an angled view. The side you draw on stays the same.
  • Drag the cube to turn the view round, and click ⌂ (top left of the cube) to go back to the normal view of everything.
  • A shape drawn on a face of your model always goes on that face, whichever side is chosen.

When you start a new, empty drawing, the cube waits in the middle of the screen and asks which side you want to draw on. Click a side, or Just start on the ground, and it moves out of the way into its corner.

5Drawing

  1. Press R and click two corners on the ground to draw a rectangle. To make it an exact size, type 40,30 and press Enter.
  2. Press P (Push/Pull), click the rectangle and move the mouse up. Type 15 and press Enter for exactly 15 mm.
  3. Draw a circle on the top face (C) and push it down through the box to cut a hole: when it reaches the far side it shows Cuts Through, so click to cut.

History: going back several steps

Every change you make is listed in the History panel on the left: Rectangle, Push/Pull, Circle, Join and so on, newest at the bottom.

  • Click a step to go back to the model as it was just after that step, like pressing Ctrl+Z many times at once. Start goes back to the beginning.
  • The steps after it turn grey. If you went back too far, click a grey step to go forward again.
  • As soon as you make a new change, the grey steps are thrown away and the new step takes their place.
  • Click the History heading to fold the panel away into a small tab, and again to open it.

Opening a file or starting a new drawing starts a new history. The last 300 steps are kept.

Changing a size after drawing

The shape you have just drawn, or the one you select, shows its sizes beside it: a rectangle its two sides, a circle or polygon its diameter (Ø), and a box its width, depth and height, coloured red, green and blue like the axes. Click a size, type the new one and press Enter. For example, if you drew a rectangle 22 mm long, click 22 mm and type 30. Esc leaves it as it was, and Ctrl+Z undoes a change.

A box keeps its left, front and bottom where they are, and its other side moves. A rectangle keeps one corner where it is. A circle grows or shrinks around its centre.

Pushing several faces at once

Say you draw a rectangle on the ground, then two lines 3 mm in from each end, so it is split into three strips. To pull all three up 10 mm in one go:

  1. Press P (Push/Pull) and Shift+click each of the three strips. They turn blue, and the status bar says 3 faces picked.
  2. Click one of them (without Shift), move up, type 10 and press Enter.

They all rise together as one solid, with no walls left between the strips, so it is ready to print. Faces you have already selected with the Select tool work the same way. (Only faces facing the same way as the one you click are pushed with it.)

Helpers while you draw

  • Snapping: the coloured dot shows what you're on: Endpoint (green), Midpoint (cyan), On Edge (red), On Face (blue).
  • Following an axis: move roughly along red, green or blue and the line snaps to it, so you can draw straight up from the ground, then across in mid-air at that height.
  • Axis lock: while drawing, the arrow keys lock to an axis: → red X, ← green Y, ↑ blue Z, ↓ unlocks.
  • From Point: while locked, hover over any corner and the line stops level with it (a dotted line shows which). For example press ↑ and hover the top of another part to draw a post exactly as tall.
  • Guide points: the Tape Measure and Find Centre leave orange points that every tool snaps to: measure 40 mm from a corner, then draw straight to the point. Shift+E clears them all.
  • Square helper: draw two lines meeting in an L and a purple cross shows the missing corner of a perfect square or rectangle.
  • Typing sizes: just start typing a number; it goes into the size box. Units like 2cm or 5'6" work too.

Ready-made shapes

Polygon (N) draws flat shapes with straight sides: a hexagon to start with, or type 3s and Enter for a triangle, 8s for an octagon. Push/Pull a hexagon to make a nut trap or a prism.

3D Shapes (D) makes a whole solid at once. Pick Ball, Cone, Cylinder or Pyramid in the panel that opens on the right, click the centre on the ground or on a face, then the radius, then the height (a ball needs only its radius). You can type each size and press Enter instead. Each shape is its own group, ready to print.

Rounding edges and offsetting

  • Round / Chamfer (J): hover a line and a blue preview shows the result (red means it can't, with the reason in the status bar). Ctrl+click does the other kind once. One line at a time works on any shape, but lines meeting at an already rounded corner can't be done one by one.
  • Round every edge (Shift+click with Round): every edge and corner of a box-like shape is rounded at once, as below. The box keeps its exact size, and prints without sharp edges. It works on shapes without dents, steps or holes; for others, round the lines one at a time.
  • Offset (F): inside, it splits the face into a rim and a middle, so you can Push/Pull the middle (a recess, a lid lip, wall thickness). Type a distance and Enter; a minus sign (-2) offsets outside. Holes in the face stay with whichever part they end up in.

Follow Me: sweeping a shape along a path

Follow Me pushes a flat shape along a route, like squeezing icing along a line. Pick the path first, then click the shape. Example: a ring.

  1. The path: press C, click the ground, type 20 and Enter: a flat circle.
  2. The shape, standing across the path: press L and click the circle's edge where it says Endpoint. Press ↑, type 4 Enter (up); →, move outwards, 3 Enter; ↑, move down, 4 Enter; then ↓ and click the first point to close the square.
  3. Select the path: press Space and click inside the circle (it turns blue).
  4. Sweep: press W and click the square. The ring is a new group; select the flat circle and press Delete if you don't need it.

A path can be one face (its outline is followed, as here) or lines: select the first and Shift+click the rest, as one unbroken chain. The shape must stand across the path (not lie along it), at the start of a line path or anywhere on an outline. A shape with a hole in it makes a tube.

Slice: cutting a solid in two

Something SketchUp doesn't have: Slice (X) cuts any closed solid in two, for example to print a tall part in two halves, to look inside, or to keep only the top of a box.

  1. Press X and hover over the shape: a red plane shows where the cut goes, and follows the mouse.
  2. Turn the plane with the arrow keys: ↑ flat (a horizontal cut), → across the red axis, ← across the green axis.
  3. Click to cut there, or type a distance from the bottom (or left / front) of the shape and press Enter for an exact cut.

Each half is closed off with a flat face where it was cut and becomes its own group: move it with M, or click it with Select and press Delete. Undo (Ctrl+Z) puts the shape back together.

Copy and paste

Select something and press Ctrl+C to copy it (or Ctrl+X to cut it). Ctrl+V pastes a copy just to the right of the original, selected and ready to move with M; paste again for more copies in a row. After a Cut, the first paste goes back where it was. Copy, Cut and Paste are also on the toolbar (grey, next to Undo) and in the right-click menu.

Groups, joining and hiding

  • Group (G): select items and group them, so they move and rotate as one piece that doesn't stick to other shapes. Ungroup with Shift+G.
  • Join (U): merges two or more objects into one solid. Click one, Shift+click the others (any face of each will do), then press U (or the merged-boxes icon, or the right-click menu). Where they overlap or touch, the walls hidden inside go, so the slicer sees a single piece. Objects that don't touch become one group that moves as one. Each must be a closed solid first (Check Model shows any gaps).
  • Subtract (S) and Intersect: see below.
  • Hide (Shift+H): hide selected faces to work on what's behind or inside them. Unhide All with Shift+U. Hidden faces are still saved and printed.

Subtract and Intersect: cutting one shape out of another

The quickest way to make a hole, a slot or a nut trap of any shape: stand the shape where the hole should be, then cut it out.

  1. Draw the object, and the shape to cut out of it standing through it (for a nut trap, a hexagon pulled up through the part with Polygon, N).
  2. With Select, click the object to keep first, then Shift+click the shape to cut out (more than one if you like).
  3. Press S (or Subtract on the toolbar or the right-click menu).

Intersect keeps only the part where the selected objects overlap, for example a ball and a box to make a dome. Both need closed solids; if nothing would be left, nothing is changed.

Flip and mirror

Select an object and click Flip / Mirror on the toolbar (or in the right-click menu):

  • Flip left ↔ right, front ↔ back, or turn upside down, where it is.
  • Mirror copy to the right, back or on top: a mirrored copy touching the original. Both are left selected, so press U to join them into one symmetrical part: draw half, mirror the other half.

Lay flat: choosing the face it prints on

Choose Lay flat on the toolbar and click the face that should go down on the printer bed. The object turns over to lie on that face, at bed level. (Also in the right-click menu: Lay flat on this face.)

3D text

Put a label on anything, raised or engraved.

  1. Click 3D Text (the T icon). A panel opens on the right: type your text, pick a font (the fonts on your computer), the letter height (of the capital letters) and the depth, and Raised or Engraved.
  2. Move over the model: the outline of the text follows the mouse. Click where the text starts, on the ground or on any face. On a wall it stands upright; on the ground it reads from the front.

Raised text is its own object: to fuse it onto the shape, select both and press U. Engraved text is cut into the face you click (it needs a closed solid).

Holes for screws, inserts and nuts

The Hole tool (the target icon) drills screw holes at sizes that work for 3D printing. Printed holes come out a little small, so its clearance holes are a touch bigger than the screw.

  1. Click Hole. In the panel, pick the screw size (M2, M2.5, M3, M4, M5, M6, M8, M10 or M12) and the kind of hole. The panel shows the exact sizes it will cut.
  2. Leave Right through ticked, or untick it and set a depth (or type a depth and press Enter; 0 means right through).
  3. Move over your model: a red circle shows the hole. Click on a face and it is drilled straight in. Snap to a guide point (Tape Measure) or a midpoint to put it in an exact place.

Threaded holes

Choose Threaded and the hole gets a real metric thread at the standard coarse pitch (M3 × 0.5, M8 × 1.25, M12 × 1.75 and so on), cut right through or to the depth you set. It is cut 0.3 mm roomier than the screw, because printed threads come out a little tight, and a small cone at the mouth helps the screw start.

Tip: threads from M6 up print well. M2 to M5 threads are very fine (ridges only 0.3 to 0.4 mm high) and wear quickly in plastic: a heat-set insert or a tapped hole is stronger for those. Print threaded holes standing upright (the hole pointing up) for the cleanest thread.

Shell: hollowing out

Turn a solid box into a tray or a case: choose Shell, type the wall thickness and press Enter (it starts at 2 mm), then click the face to leave open, e.g. the top.

  • Shift+click to pick more faces to leave open first, then click the last one.
  • Ctrl+click hollows it with no opening (a sealed hollow inside, which slicers print as an empty space).
  • It works on box-like shapes: boxes, cylinders, prisms. For a shape with dents or steps, Offset a face inwards and push the middle in instead.

6Making it ready to print

Click Check Model (the shield icon). It checks five things and marks any problem right on the model:

CheckIf there's a problem
WatertightGaps are shown with thick red lines. Click Fill holes, or trace the gap closed with the Line tool.
Faces point outwardInside-out faces get orange outlines. Click Fix directions.
No loose linesLines that aren't part of a face won't print. Click Delete loose lines.

Sending it to your slicer

Click Export 3MF or Export STL, then open the file in Cura, PrusaSlicer, Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer or any other slicer. Sizes are always in millimetres. If something is selected, only the selection is exported. In a 3MF file each separate piece becomes its own part, so the slicer can place them separately.

Tip: if a part arrives in the slicer far too big or small, it was drawn with the wrong Units setting. Use Resize to set its real size.

7Saving and opening

  • Save (Ctrl+S) makes a .3mf file. Any slicer can print it, and it opens again in WaughForm 3D fully editable.
  • Open (Ctrl+O) opens .3mf and .stl files, including ones from other programs: flat areas become editable faces.
  • Your work is also saved automatically as you go and comes back the next time you open the program.
  • Export PNG saves a picture of the view; Export OBJ is for other 3D programs.

8The Parts Library

Click Parts. The library holds the electronic parts you put in boxes: their size, the holes and sockets that matter, and their pins. It comes with an ESP32-S3 board, an INMP441 microphone, a MAX98357A amplifier, a 3 W speaker, a TP4056 charger, two batteries and a slide switch. Your own parts are saved on your computer.

Browsing ready-made parts

The quickest way to add a part: click Browse parts… at the top of the Parts Library (or Browse over 2,000 ready-made parts in the Enclosure Wizard). Over 2,000 parts come with WaughForm 3D, with their pins, sizes and pictures, so no internet or Fritzing is needed.

  • Search by part number (HC-SR04, MPU6050) or simply by what it does: gyroscope, temperature humidity, air quality, screen, relay, GPS… The best matches come first.
  • All kinds narrows the list to sensors, boards, displays, motors, sound, power and so on, to scroll through.
  • Click a part to see its picture, size, number of pins and description.
  • In the web-page version (WaughForm_3D.html in a browser), the first Browse parts… asks for the file parts-library.json that comes with it. The browser keeps it, so it only asks once.
  • Add to my box saves the part and puts it straight into the Enclosure Wizard. Open to check first opens it as a new part to look over (and measure against the real board) before you save it.

Adding a part from a photo

  1. Measure the part with a ruler or calipers: length, width and height, in millimetres.
  2. Take a photo looking straight down on it, with a plain background.
  3. Click + New part, type a name and the measurements.
  4. Click Top photo…, choose the picture and click the board's four corners in order: top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left. If a corner is rounded, click where it would be if it were square. The photo is straightened to scale; if it was taken the other way round, it's turned to match, and Turn photo 180° fixes upside-down.
  5. Mark the important bits: click a + button above the picture, then click (or drag) on the photo.
  6. Click Save part.

Pins and ready-made part files

The Pins table at the bottom lists each pin and its role (power, ground, I2S clock, trigger, any signal…). The Wiring window works from these roles. Click + Pin to add one by hand.

Quicker: someone may already have made a part file (.fzpz) for your board, with all its pins, its size and a picture.

  1. In the blue Fill in automatically box click Search for a part file (.fzpz)…. Your web browser searches for the part name plus "fzpz".
  2. Download a .fzpz that matches your board.
  3. Drag the file onto the part picture (or click Import pins… › Use a .fzpz file…). Import pins… also lists the same ready-made parts to search (sensors, boards, modules, displays…), from the parts library that comes with WaughForm 3D, so you don't need Fritzing or the internet. If Fritzing is installed, its own parts are listed instead.

A board drawn standing up is turned automatically so its long side runs left to right. Always check the pin roles, and measure the board: those pictures keep the pin spacing exact but are sometimes drawn smaller than the real board.

9Filling in a part automatically

This is the only feature that uses the internet, and only when you click it. An AI looks the part up and fills in a draft, which you check before saving.

Choosing the AI and adding a key

Look up online

  1. Click + New part, then Look up online… and type the exact part number (e.g. HY-SRF05 or ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1).
  2. The AI searches the web, reads the maker's documentation and fills in the sizes, holes and sockets (pins come from a part file, chapter 8). The yellow bar lists anything it wasn't sure of, with links to its sources.
  3. In the desktop app it also downloads product photos (click one, then its 4 corners, to line it up) and reads any dimension drawing or datasheet it found for exact positions. The drawing appears in the top-right corner for checking.
  4. Check every number, fix anything wrong, and click Save part.

If the look-up can't find the part, use Search images myself… to find a dimension drawing in Google Images, copy it, and press Ctrl+V in the part (or use Read a drawing…). The AI then reads the drawing.

Always check: AI can misread drawings, and cheap modules come in several versions with different hole positions. Only the part number (or the drawing and part name) is sent to the AI; on Google's free plan Google may use it to improve its products.

10The Enclosure Wizard

Click Enclosure. Tell it which parts go in the box, and it designs the smallest printable box (base and lid) that fits them, with all the right holes.

  1. Parts: add them from the list; set how many, and (if you like) which wall a part faces. Remove a part with its red ✕.
  2. Power: USB cable only, or a battery inside (a battery, charger and on/off switch are added for you).
  3. Style and Lid (see below), and the Box size: the smallest that fits, at least a size, or an exact size.
  4. Check the layout. Drag a part to move it, or drag a wall part onto another wall; Rotate part (or R) turns the selected part. The list underneath shows ✓ for what's fine and ! or ✗ for what isn't. Auto arrange starts again.
  5. Click Generate base + lid. The base and lid appear as two groups, checked and ready to print.
StyleBest for
CompactWires soldered in place. The smallest, neatest box.
Prototype / DupontStill experimenting: room for push-on jumper wires, wider gaps and a cable slot.
RuggedThick 3 mm walls, a screw-down lid with a gasket groove, and mounting ears.

Lids: push-fit (a lip that slides in), snap-fit (clicks on), or screws (4 × M3 self-tapping into corner posts). Base and lid are separate pieces, so print the lid first to check the fit.

The rules it follows: sockets go against a wall so the hole lines up; microphones face out, away from the speaker; antennas stay clear; chargers sit near batteries; nothing touches anything else.

No supports needed: wall parts (e.g. ultrasonic sensors) sit on standoffs with 45° braces, and press-in rings get a 45° chamfer, so set supports to None in your slicer.

11Wiring

Click Wiring (or Wiring… in the Enclosure Wizard). For the parts in your box it works out what wire goes where, using each part's pins.

  • Safe pins: on the ESP32-S3 it never uses the pins that would stop it working: GPIO 0, 3, 45, 46 (start-up), 19/20 (USB), 43/44 (serial), 35–37 (memory on N16R8 boards) and 38/48 (RGB LED).
  • Power: USB, or battery → charger → switch → everything else, with every ground joined (to the charger's OUT−, never B−).
  • Warnings for anything risky, such as a 5 V signal into a 3.3 V pin (it tells you which resistors to add) or a speaker wire to ground.

The diagram

Each board with its pins and coloured wires: red 5 V, orange battery, pink 3.3 V, black GND, other colours for signals. Drag boards to tidy it. Hover a wire in the list to find it in the picture. Save picture saves it as a PNG.

The wiring list and code

Tick each wire as you solder it; Save wiring list saves it to print. Code pins gives the matching pin settings for ESPHome and Arduino, ready to copy.

Before you power up: check every connection against the labels printed on your own boards.

12Programming your board (ESPHome)

If the controller is an ESP32 (or ESP8266) board, the ESPHome device tab in the Wiring window writes the whole file ESPHome needs to program it, using the pins from this wiring, so the board and the wires always match.

  1. Open Wiring and click the ESPHome device tab.
  2. Give the device a name (it's how Home Assistant shows it) and choose what it's for:
    • Sensor node: reports its readings (distance, temperature, motion, button, battery…). Works with Home Assistant, or on its own with its own web page. Set how often it reads, and the "Something close" alarm distance for an ultrasonic sensor.
    • Voice assistant: needs an I2S microphone and amplifier with a speaker. Pick the wake word; it talks to Home Assistant.
  3. Answer the other questions shown: battery-low level, what the button does, and whether the board's own RGB LED is a status light.
  4. Click Send to ESPHome. The device goes straight into ESPHome Device Builder on this computer, and ESPHome opens. (If a device of that name is already there, it asks before replacing it.) Check with ESPHome checks the file first, the same as Validate; Copy YAML and Save .yaml… are there if you want the file itself.
  5. In ESPHome, find your device, plug the board in by USB and click Install. ESPHome builds the program and puts it on the board. Your Wi-Fi name and password come from ESPHome's Secrets; WaughForm 3D tells you if they're missing.

Your Wi-Fi name and password are not in the file: it uses ESPHome's Secrets (wifi_ssid, wifi_password). Parts it knows get their own section (I2S microphone and amplifier, ultrasonic sensors, DHT11/DHT22 and DS18B20 temperature sensors, BME280, BMP280, AHT10/20 and SHT3x sensors, buttons, PIR motion sensors, LEDs, relays, buzzers, battery level) straight away. Any other part, such as one you imported from a part file, is handled by your AI: the tab lists it under "needs the AI", and one click on Make it with AI writes its code from the part's name, its part-file description and its pins. You don't need to know what the module is or how it works.

Telling it what to do

Light rule (no internet needed): tick it and fill in the sentence, for example The status light is red while Temperature is above 28 °C, otherwise green. It works with any reading the device has (temperature, humidity, distance, battery voltage…) and the board's RGB status light, or a plain LED (on/off).

What should it do? (uses your AI key): optional. If you leave it empty, Make it with AI just makes every part work and report its readings. Or describe it in your own words, for example "Turn the relay on when the probe is over 30 °C and off again below 27 °C", and click Make it with AI. Your AI (Gemini or Claude, set up in Parts) adds it to the file without touching the pins or Wi-Fi. ESPHome on this computer then checks the result; if it finds a mistake, the error goes back to the AI to fix, up to three tries. The panel shows ✓ when it passes, with the AI's own notes on what it added.

Always read an AI-written file through before installing it. Back to the plain file undoes it, and changing any answer goes back to the plain file too.

Battery level: with a battery, the wiring adds two 100 kΩ resistors from the battery + (after the switch) to GND, with the middle going to a measuring pin, so the board can report its battery voltage.

13Sharing with other people

  • Drawings: send the .3mf file you saved. Slicers print it, and WaughForm 3D opens it fully editable.
  • Parts: in Parts click Export… and send the .json file; they click Import…. If your parts have photos you can export with or without them. Photos downloaded from websites belong to whoever took them, so choose Export without photos when sharing.
  • The program: send the .exe with this manual, the README and the checksums file, or simply send the link to the GitHub download page. Each person sets up their own AI key if they want the online feature; keys are never shared.
